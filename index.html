Part I / 01

Four Modes of Convergence

A sequence of random variables Xₙ may approach a random variable X in several mathematically distinct ways.

1. Convergence in Probability

P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0   for all ε > 0

For large n, the probability of a noticeable error becomes small. The sequence can still occasionally have large deviations.

Key role: the Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN).

2. Almost Sure Convergence (a.s.)

P(limₙ Xₙ = X) = 1

Along almost every individual infinite realization, the sequence eventually approaches its limit.

Key role: the Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN).

3. Mean-Square Convergence (L²)

E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0

Squared approximation error vanishes on average. It controls both bias and variance when the limit is a constant.

Key role: mean-square consistency of sample means and estimators.

4. Convergence in Distribution

F₍Xₙ₎(t) → F₍X₎(t)

Distribution functions converge at every continuity point of the limiting CDF; individual sample paths need not approach one another.

Key role: the Central Limit Theorem (CLT).

Which implications are valid?

Almost sure ⇒ In probability ⇒ In distribution
L² ⇒ In probability ⇒ In distribution
Important distinction: Almost sure convergence and L² convergence do not imply each other in general. Reverse implications are generally false unless additional assumptions are made. If the distributional limit is a constant, convergence in distribution does imply convergence in probability.

Part I / 03

Why Does √n Keep Appearing?

For independent observations with common mean μ and finite variance σ², variance adds:

Var(Σᵢ Xᵢ) = nσ²   ⇒   Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²/n

Standard deviation is the square root of variance, so the standard error of the mean is σ/√n. Multiplying estimation error by √n therefore puts the fluctuations on a non-degenerate, order-one scale.

√n (X̄ₙ − μ)/σ   ⇒ᵈ   N(0, 1)
Role of independence: for dependent variables, Var(Σᵢ Xᵢ) = Σᵢ Var(Xᵢ) + 2Σᵢ<ⱼ Cov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ). Covariance terms may change the rate; √n scaling is not universal. Other regularity conditions are also required for a CLT.
Simulated normalized sample meansStandard normal density

Each repetition averages n independent Uniform(0,1) random variables, then computes Z = √n(X̄ − 0.5)/√(1/12). The curve is the standard normal density. For n = 1, the original distribution is uniform; as n increases, the standardized sampling distribution tends toward normality.

LLN: Consistency

X̄ₙ → μ: the unscaled sample mean concentrates near the population mean as n grows.

CLT: Distribution of Errors

√n(X̄ₙ − μ)/σ ⇒ N(0,1): standardized random error tends to a normal distribution, enabling approximate confidence intervals and tests.

Part II / 01

Conditional Probability & Bayes' Theorem

Conditional probability describes how the probability of an event changes after learning another event occurred. In statistics, it underlies likelihoods, predictive probabilities, and evidence-based inference.

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B),   P(B) > 0

Bayes' Theorem

P(H | D) = P(D | H) P(H) / P(D)
01 · Prior P(H)Belief about a hypothesis H before seeing the current data D.
02 · Likelihood P(D | H)How compatible the observed data are with a specified hypothesis.
03 · Posterior P(H | D)Updated belief about the hypothesis after observing data.

The denominator P(D) is the evidence or marginal likelihood: it normalizes the posterior across possible hypotheses.

P(D) = P(D | H)P(H) + P(D | ¬H)P(¬H)
Likelihood is not a posterior: P(D | H) and P(H | D) condition in opposite directions and generally have different values.

Applications, Advantages & Limitations

Frequentist Asymptotic Theory

Applications: sample-mean consistency, approximate confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and asymptotic normality of estimators.

Strength: LLN and CLT provide broad theoretical guarantees under suitable conditions.

Limitation: small samples, strong dependence, infinite variance, or violated assumptions can undermine normal approximations.

Bayesian Statistical Inference

Applications: screening, model comparison, parameter estimation, forecasting, and sequential updating as evidence arrives.

Strength: incorporates previous information and expresses uncertainty through posterior probabilities.

Limitation: priors can matter with limited data; likelihood misspecification and computational demands can affect conclusions.

Final Coursework Conclusions

Convergence: Different modes describe different meanings of approaching a limit. The Weak LLN primarily concerns convergence in probability; the Strong LLN concerns almost sure convergence; the CLT establishes convergence in distribution of normalized fluctuations. For i.i.d. data with finite variance, sample means also converge in mean square.

The √n factor: Independence removes covariance terms, giving variance of the sample mean proportional to 1/n and standard error proportional to 1/√n. That explains the normalization used in classical CLT-based inference.

Bayesian inference: Bayes' theorem combines prior beliefs with the likelihood of observed evidence to form a posterior distribution. Conclusions depend on both the data and modeling choices; strong claims require checking assumptions and prior sensitivity.
